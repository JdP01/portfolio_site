Data and decision-making

Foundations: data science supplies principles for extracting useful knowledge from data; data mining applies techniques embodying those principles. Data and the organizational capability to use it are complementary strategic assets. A large collection without reliable definitions, access controls, expertise, and an actionable decision may create costs rather than value. Useful data can require deliberate acquisition, instrumentation, consent, and ongoing maintenance.

Data-driven decision-making supports both discoveries, such as identifying an underserved segment, and repeated decisions, such as routing thousands of service requests. Studies associating data-driven organizations with greater productivity do not automatically establish causation: management quality, industry, and resources may explain some differences. Randomized interventions or credible causal designs are needed to support stronger causal claims.

Six stages, many feedback loops

CRISP-DM means Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining. Its stages organize work, not a one-way waterfall. Evaluation can expose a target-definition problem; preparation can reveal that new data must be collected.

CRISP-DM stages and concrete outputs
StageQuestions and deliverables
Business understandingDefine the decision, beneficiaries, constraints, costs, baseline, and measurable success.
Data understandingInventory sources, inspect distributions and provenance, and assess representativeness and label quality.
Data preparationJoin, clean, encode, and construct point-in-time features without contaminating evaluation.
ModelingChoose task-appropriate algorithms, fit parameters, and tune candidate configurations.
EvaluationAssess generalization, uncertainty, operational costs, and whether the original business goal is met.
DeploymentIntegrate decisions into workflows; assign owners, monitor outcomes, and plan retraining or retirement.

Exploratory analytics resembles research and development because useful signal is uncertain. This does not make engineering inappropriate: reproducible pipelines, versioning, testing, reliable serving, and monitoring turn discoveries into durable services. Research and engineering complement each other. A reasonable project can end with evidence that modeling is not worthwhile.

Queries, warehouses, statistics, and learning

A database query retrieves or aggregates records according to specified logic. Queries can support discovery, but ordinarily do not themselves learn a predictive rule. OLAP supports interactive slicing, aggregation, and drill-down across dimensions; it is not inherently a predictive-learning algorithm, nor must every interface be graphical. A warehouse integrates historical enterprise data for analysis; centralization alone does not ensure consistent definitions or quality.

Machine learning emphasizes algorithms that learn from experience and includes applications beyond business data mining. Knowledge discovery in databases, or KDD, commonly describes a broader process of selection, preparation, mining, interpretation, and evaluation; terminology overlaps rather than marking rigid disciplinary borders. Predictive analysis asks whether a model works on new cases. Explanatory analysis seeks interpretable relationships; explaining an association still does not identify an intervention's causal effect.

Evidence and uncertainty

Summary statistics describe data. Means can be appropriate for totals and expected costs, while medians better describe a typical observation under strong skew; examine both alongside spread. A frequentist 95% confidence-interval procedure covers the fixed population parameter in 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions. It is not a statement that 95% of individual outcomes lie inside the interval.

A hypothesis-test p-value measures how incompatible the observed statistic is with a specified null model, not the probability the null is true. Testing many patterns after searching data creates false discoveries. Pre-specification, independent confirmation, and appropriate family-wise or false-discovery-rate procedures help. With 20 independent tests of true nulls at significance 0.05, the probability of at least one false rejection is 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64.2%.

Worked example: a retention pilot

Worked example: randomly assign 1,000 eligible customers to outreach and 1,000 to control. Observe 140 versus 100 renewals: rates are 14% and 10%, an estimated increase of four percentage points. The approximate standard error is √[(0.14 × 0.86)/1000 + (0.10 × 0.90)/1000] ≈ 0.0145. A normal-approximation 95% interval is 0.04 ± 1.96 × 0.0145, or roughly 1.2–6.8 percentage points.

At 100 units of contribution per incremental renewal and two units per contact, estimated incremental contribution per contacted customer is 0.04 × 100 − 2 = 2 units. This calculation assumes those economics apply and excludes implementation costs. Deployment should verify actual incremental value, capacity, customer impact, and changing conditions, not merely report predictive accuracy.

Pitfalls and worked examples

A model that predicts churn is not automatically a model of who can be persuaded to stay. Prediction quality and treatment effectiveness are different questions.
Why can a technically accurate model fail commercially?

Its predictions may arrive too late, trigger an ineffective action, exceed staffing capacity, or optimize the wrong outcome. Evaluate the complete decision process against a meaningful baseline.

When should the team return to business understanding?

Whenever evidence changes the feasible decision: missing labels, unavailable features, unacceptable costs, or a mismatch between modeled outcomes and stakeholder needs.
